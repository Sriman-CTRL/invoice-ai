How to invoice as a freelancer in India

A good invoice is boring on purpose: the client can see who is billing, for what, how much, and by when to pay. Here is what to put on it and how to avoid the usual mix-ups.

What a clear invoice contains

Whether or not you charge GST, every invoice should answer the questions your client's accountant will ask. Put these on it:

  • Your details: your name or business name, address, email or phone, and your GSTIN if you are registered.
  • Your client's details: the name they want on the bill, their address, and their GSTIN if they have one and want input credit.
  • An invoice number and a date. Numbers must be unique and ideally run in order (see below).
  • What you did: a line for each piece of work, with quantity and rate. "Logo design, 1 x 15,000" beats "Services".
  • The money: subtotal, any discount, GST shown separately if you charge it, and the total you are asking for.
  • How and when to pay: a due date, your UPI ID or bank details (account name, number, IFSC).
  • Notes: anything agreed, such as "50% advance already received".

If you charge GST, the law has a detailed list of what a tax invoice must show (for example the place of supply and the tax split). Instead of memorising it, use invoicing software that includes those fields, and ask your CA to look at one sample invoice once. Do you need to register for GST?

Registration depends on your yearly turnover crossing a limit set in the GST law. The limit is different for different kinds of supplies and for different states, and some situations (for example, certain online or inter-state supplies) have their own rules. Because this decides whether you must charge your clients GST at all, take the number from the official source and not from a blog.

We have not printed the registration limit here. On 24 Nov 2026 we could not open the official page that states it, and a number from a blog is not good enough for something that affects your tax.

Where to check: the GST portal at gst.gov.in and the GST Council site gstcouncil.gov.in. Or ask a CA, which usually costs less than a mistake. If you are registered, the rate depends on your service. The GST Council's decisions of September 2025, in force from 22 September 2025, use a standard rate of 18%, a merit rate of 5% and a special rate of 40% for a few items; which one applies to your work is a classification question for your CA.

If you are not registered, do not charge GST. Invoice the plain amount, and write "Not registered under GST" in the notes so the client's accountant understands.

Number your invoices simply

  • Pick one pattern and stick to it, for example 2026-27/001, 2026-27/002. Starting a fresh series each financial year is common.
  • Never reuse a number, and never edit a sent invoice quietly. If something was wrong, issue a credit note and a new invoice so the paper trail is clean.
  • Do not use a guessable number as the public link to pay. Paivo's pay links use a long random id for that reason.

Choose payment terms you can live with

Terms are how many days the client has to pay. Common choices are due on receipt, 7, 15 or 30 days. Practical rules:

  • New client, small job: ask for an advance (many freelancers ask for half) and the rest before you hand over final files.
  • Big project: split into milestones, each with its own invoice.
  • Repeat client with a company accounts team: they may need 30 days. Agree it before you start, and put it on the invoice.
  • Write the due date, not just "Net 15". A date cannot be argued with.

Make it easy to pay

Most late payments are not refusals but friction: the client has to find your bank details, ask for the UPI ID, or work out the amount. Put a UPI QR and bank details on the same page as the invoice, and say what to send you afterwards (the UTR, the 12-digit transaction reference, so you can match the payment). Keep a simple routine

  1. Send the invoice the day the work is delivered or the milestone is reached, not at month end.
  2. Send a polite reminder the day after the due date (see our guide on following up on late payments).
  3. Mark it paid only after you have seen the money in your bank.
  4. Save the PDF and the payment proof for the year-end.

Common invoicing mistakes

  • Billing in the wrong name. Ask the client for the exact legal name and address they want on the invoice before you create it. Fixing it later means a credit note and a new invoice.
  • Mixing personal and business money. If clients pay into the same account you use for groceries, year-end becomes painful. Use one account or UPI ID for work, even if it is a savings account.
  • No due date. "Please pay soon" is not a date. Pick one and write it.
  • Sending a screenshot instead of an invoice. A PDF or a link with a number and a date is easier to file, and easier for the client to forward to their accounts team.
  • Forgetting what was already paid. If you took an advance, show it on the final invoice, so the balance due is clear.
  • Invoicing a client abroad. The tax treatment of services for clients outside India is a question for your CA. Ask before the first invoice, not after.

A quick pre-send check

Before you press send, read the invoice once as the client's accountant: is the name right, is the amount right, is the due date there, can I pay without asking a question? Two minutes here saves a week of messages.

This is general information, not tax or legal advice. Check with a CA before you act on it.
